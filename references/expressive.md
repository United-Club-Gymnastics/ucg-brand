# Expressive design

Merch, event graphics, social campaigns and big moments like Nationals. Based on the *UCG Guide to Expressive Design* (Build Design Studio, 2026), with UCG's later decisions: AA contrast still applies, and the expressive colors are a fixed list of six.

The core system ([brand guidelines](../BRAND.md)) keeps UCG recognizable: the same logo, colors and type every time, so people know it's us on sight. Expressive design builds on top of that system without breaking it. It brings energy for a specific moment (a season, an event, a merch drop) and gives members something to rally around. The skill is knowing how far to push while still looking like UCG.

## Where expression lives

Not every surface is the place to get expressive.

**Green light: push it.**

- **Merch and apparel.** Tees, hats, tanks and gear are made to be worn loud. Push the color and let the type carry the energy.
- **Event graphics.** Banners, signage and hype pieces for one event can flex hard for the moment.
- **Social campaigns.** Seasonal pushes, drops and one-off moments. Go bigger than you would on the everyday feed.
- **Big-moment collateral.** Nationals, launches, milestones.

**Red light: hold the line.**

- **Core logo lockups.** The logo stays as drawn.
- **Official communications.** Policies, rules, registration info, emails, Google Docs, Slides and Sheets use the core system. Clarity beats flair.
- **Signage and wayfinding that has to last.** Permanent, functional pieces stay on-system.
- **Anything people rely on to find us year after year,** like the website, avatars and document headers.

**Mixed pieces** (e.g. a Nationals hype post that also gives registration dates): the facts (dates, deadlines, links, prices) stay in core colors and plain, readable type. Expressive color, pattern and decoration go around them.

## The anchor elements

Every expressive piece needs at least one recognizable **anchor** so people know where it came from. There are three. Keep at least one locked and you can play with the rest.

- **Logo.** The strongest anchor. You can change the color it plays, add a stroke, or let it sit big in the background. You can't redraw it. As long as the mark is there and unchanged in shape, people know it's us.
- **Color.** Where you get the most room. Pull from the brand palette and the expressive colors below to shift the mood of a piece while staying on-brand. Off-palette colors are the fastest way to look like someone else.
- **Type.** Greed Condensed (or Saira Condensed) carries the voice. Scale it big, stack it, run it vertically, let it drive the layout. Keep the typeface on-system and the personality comes through on its own.

## What you can do with the mark

In expressive work only (merch, campaigns, event graphics):

- **Recolor** the logo or mark in any one brand-palette color (for example Purple on charcoal), with enough contrast to read: 3:1 against its background.
- **Add a stroke** or use an outlined version. Keep the stroke thinner than the gap between neighbouring V's (under 4% of the mark's width; about 3% looks right), so every outline stays separate and never touches the next one. Leave room around the mark so nothing crops the stroke.
- **Use it huge in the background,** cropped off the edge of a shirt or graphic, behind the main logo or type.
- **Add texture** (a distressed or print-worn finish) over the shape, without changing the shape.
- **Repeat it** in a pattern, like the event pattern below.

Never redraw, stretch, rotate, rebuild or rearrange the mark or wordmark, and never set "UCG" in a font. Use the files in `assets/logos/` as drawn. In everyday materials, stick to the four colorways (navy, light, white, black) and no outlines (see [`BRAND.md`](../BRAND.md)).

## Expressive colors

Six extra colors for expressive work only, on top of the brand palette. They are not part of the everyday palette: never use them in official communications, wayfinding, UI, Google Docs, Slides or Sheets.

| Swatch | Hex | Text on it | Contrast |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yellow | `#FFEF77` | Navy | 12.3:1 |
| Coral | `#FF988A` | Navy | 6.9:1 |
| Green | `#C0E388` | Navy | 10.0:1 |
| Sky blue | `#8BB7E3` | Navy | 6.8:1 |
| Blue | `#336494` | White | 6.2:1 |
| Indigo | `#504A83` | White | 7.9:1 |

- Use only these six (plus the brand palette). Don't pick shades in between.
- Text on them still meets AA: Navy on the four light swatches, White on Blue and Indigo. Never White on the light four or Navy on the dark two.
- The color wheel (`assets/palette/ucg-color-wheel.png`) shows how they sit inside the brand palette: brand primary and secondary colors on the outer ring, expressive colors in the inner ring.

## Learn from athletic brands

Look at how big athletic brands stretch their identity across merch, events and campaigns, and notice what stays the same.

1. **The mark never disappears.** On event and apparel work the logo is always in the frame. It might change color, carry a stroke or play a background role, but it's never redrawn. The fun happens around it.
2. **The type does the heavy lifting.** Strong, bold type is what makes the work feel like theirs. Colors shift with the context, but the typographic voice holds.
3. **Color flexes, identity holds.** Expressive pieces reach into a wider color range for energy and mood, while the core elements underneath keep it grounded.

## Before it ships

- **Do keep an anchor:** the logo, the colors or the type doing recognizable work.
- **Do pull color from the approved range:** the brand palette plus the six expressive colors, nothing else.
- **Do let type carry the energy.** Strong type is the fastest route to on-brand personality.
- **Do keep every piece of text at AA contrast,** and keep the facts in core colors and plain type.
- **Don't redraw the mark.** Recolor it, stroke it, move it, crop it big in the background; never rebuild it.
- **Don't let outlines touch.** A stroke as wide as the gap between V's (about 4% of the mark's width) merges them.
- **Do hire a designer** for anything big or public-facing, like apparel and large print runs. Questions about whether something is on-brand? Ask your team lead or department head.
